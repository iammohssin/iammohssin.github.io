A short version of a long story

I build the connective tissue of software: the API nine other things depend on, the relay that lets two devices that shouldn't be able to talk to each other talk anyway, the auth system that has to be right every single time. That's been the throughline across five-plus years of full-stack work — TypeScript, React, Angular, Node.js, and whatever database the problem actually calls for.

Day to day, I lead backend development at ForunPoint — Node.js and NestJS APIs for a multi-service platform spanning food delivery, gift delivery, ride-hailing, and car rental, plus the admin/vendor dashboards and payment integrations (Stripe, PayPal, JazzCash, EasyPaisa, 2Checkout) that keep it running. I started there as a full-stack developer in 2023 and now mentor the team building it. Outside of that, I build open-source infrastructure for the same reason I build it at work: because the unglamorous parts — migrations, session handling, a native platform bridge nobody wants to touch — are the parts that make the glamorous parts actually work.

Recent nights-and-weekends work includes AuthLume, a self-hostable auth platform built to replace six SDKs with one API, and SimShare, which keeps a single phone line alive across two physically separate phones over WebRTC. I like problems shaped like that.

Lately that list has grown to include AI — wiring chatbots and LLM-backed features into real products, not demo scripts. And on mobile, SimShare pushed me further into native territory than a typical React Native app ever does: CallKit on iOS, Android's Telecom stack, and a Shizuku-based system service to capture call audio Android normally locks away from third-party apps entirely.

REST APIs and third-party integrations — payments, maps, cloud storage — documented with Swagger/OpenAPI so the next developer isn't guessing.

React Native and Expo apps for iOS and Android — not just CRUD screens. When the platform's own APIs get in the way, I go down into native modules: CallKit, Android's Telecom stack, Shizuku-level system permissions, the kind of thing SimShare needed to make two phones share one phone line.
